What is a zonotent?

A zonotope is a convex, centrally symmetric polytope Z = Z(c, G) = \{c + G\beta : \|\beta\|_\infty \le 1\}, with center c and generators given by the columns of G. Zonotopes are closed under affine maps and Minkowski sums, the two main operations of reachability analysis for uncertain linear systems, and both operations need only matrix arithmetic, which is why a zonotope is a common robust representation of every state a system can reach. A zonotent treats Z as the support of a probability density f, which integrates to 1 over Z. Roughly speaking, the density says which states of Z are likely. A Gaussian zonotent puts a scaled Gaussian, truncated to Z, on top of a uniform wall:

f(x) = w + \alpha\,\mathcal N(x;\mu,\Sigma)\ \text{ on } Z,w = \frac{\eta}{\operatorname{vol}(Z)},\qquad \alpha = \frac{1-\eta}{\nu(Z)},\nu(Z) = \int_Z \mathcal N(x;\mu,\Sigma)\,dx.

The wall share \eta \in [0,1] is the fraction of mass in the wall, so \eta = 0 gives a Gaussian truncated to Z and \eta = 1 the uniform zonotent. Note that the mean \mu need not lie in Z, and that (\mu, \Sigma) are parameters, not the mean and covariance of the density.

Background and motivation

Reachability analysis typically propagates robust sets such as zonotopes, which bound the reachable states but assign no probability to them. Gaussians assign probability, but since they are unbounded, some of it falls on states the system can never reach. A Gaussian zonotent benefits both representations, since it keeps the physical bounds of the zonotope while its density shows which reachable states are likely.

Each step of reachability analysis appends the generators of the noise to the zonotope, so the cost of computing with it grows over time. The number of generators per dimension, called the order of the zonotope, is usually reduced by replacing the zonotope with a larger one that has fewer generators, and conservatism enters here, because the added unreachable states compound over many steps. Probabilistic order reduction, our main contribution, instead uses the density to discard regions that hold at most a chosen share of the probability, called the budget, with a bound on how much this changes any probability computed afterward. For example, a sum of twenty disturbances, each uniform on [−1, 1], is supported on [−20, 20] but leaves [−10, 10] with probability below 10−4.

In numerical experiments on uncertain linear systems, Gaussian zonotents matched Monte Carlo estimates of collision probabilities as closely as untruncated Gaussians. These probabilities are approximate, so for guarantees we propagate a Gaussian overbound, a Gaussian zonotent whose density lies above that of the true state, and bound the probability that the true state ever leaves a sequence of sets, such as reduced supports or the states in which the system is safe. This first-exit bound certified, for the true system, the passage of a robot through a gap 42% narrower than the exact reachable zonotope allows, and it bounded the probability of spilling a sliding cup of water with an unknown fill depth, in a linear slosh model, while accepting motions that worst-case reachability rejected.

Zonotents can bound the chance the water spills

A cup of water slides 40 cm across a table along a minimum-jerk path that takes τ seconds, and its fill depth is unknown (7.5 to 8.5 cm). A zonotope alone contains every state the slosh model can reach, so it flags a spill even for moves that almost never spill. The density of a zonotent shows that those states are unlikely, and a first-exit bound on a Gaussian overbound bounds the probability that the water spills at any time during the move, which lets a planner pick the fastest move that meets a risk budget (e.g., at most a 1% chance of spilling). Minkowski sums by moment matching

Adding independent noise Y to a state X takes the Minkowski sum Z_1 \oplus Z_2 of the supports, and the density of X+Y is the convolution of the two densities, which is not a Gaussian zonotent. So that propagation stays within the family, the convolution is approximated by the moment-matched sum, which is the Gaussian zonotent on Z_1 \oplus Z_2, with no wall, whose own mean and covariance are exactly those of X+Y, namely \hat\mu_1+\hat\mu_2 and \hat\Sigma_1+\hat\Sigma_2. In the demo, its parameters are found by a fixed-point iteration. In higher dimensions, where that iteration is costly, the sum under mild truncation sets them to the target mean and covariance directly. Note that the moment-matched sum carries no guarantee, so the demo compares it with the exact convolution.

Probabilistic order reduction

Every Minkowski sum adds generators, so the support of x_{t+1} = A x_t + d_t after t steps has 2 + 3t generators in the system below. Classical order reduction encloses the zonotope in one of lower order, which can only grow the support. A density instead allows parts of the support that carry little probability to be discarded, up to a budget \zeta, in three forms:

  • Outer: the reduced support Z' contains Z, so it keeps every reachable state and all of the probability, and conservatism enters only through the added volume. Here, we use the interval hull of Z in the whitened frame of \Sigma.
  • Inner: the reduced support Z' lies inside Z and keeps \int_{Z'} f \ge 1-\zeta. Every probability \int_U f is then bounded as \int_{U\cap Z'} f \le \int_U f \le \int_{U\cap Z'} f + \zeta. Here, a maximum-volume template is centered at the mode (or at c when the mode is on the boundary of Z) and shrunk to the budget. Note that this reduction misses its budget when the template holds less than 1-\zeta of the mass.
  • Free: the reduced support is the confidence parallelotope Z(\mu, r_\zeta L) with \Sigma = LL^\top, which is computed in closed form and need neither contain nor lie inside Z. When Z is symmetric about \mu, the Gaussian correlation inequality certifies that it keeps 1-\zeta of the probability.

Experiments

E1 · Validation

Order reduction across dimension

Which reductions meet their budget along propagated states from ℝ2 to ℝ10, and what does each cost in volume, accuracy, and time?

In each dimension, we propagate one random linear system (spectral radius 0.9) with uniform or truncated Gaussian noise for 20 steps by moment-matched sums, and reduce it after every step with ζ = 10−3.

  • The free reduction met its budget at every step, in every dimension, and with both noise models, in 1 to 2 ms per reduction (lowest kept share 0.99894 among 240).
  • Outer reductions keep every state, but their volume compounds. With truncated Gaussian noise in ℝ10, the enclosing parallelotope reached 1.4×1012 times the 99.9% confidence ellipsoid, and the PCA reduction 7.4×1019.
  • Inner reductions met their budgets only in low dimensions, and with uniform noise missed them at every step from ℝ3 on.
  • Every moment-matched sum under mild truncation took at most 40 ms, on supports with up to 210 generators in ℝ10, whereas the fixed-point iteration took 3 s for four generators in ℝ4.

E2

Approximate probabilities

Along an affine dynamical system and with bounded noise, how close to the truth are the probabilities of a Gaussian zonotent, and how do they compare with those of probabilistic zonotopes?

Collision. The largest errors of the moment-matched zonotent over t = 0…5 were 0.004 with uniform noise and 0.007 with truncated Gaussian noise, the same as those of the untruncated Gaussian, and reducing the support after every step raised them by up to 0.002. At t = 5 the reduced support had area 0.52 with truncated Gaussian noise, against 2.56 for the exact reachable zonotope and 4.61 for its outer reduction.

Probabilistic zonotopes. We use the system of Althoff et al. (2009), discretized over 250 steps, with the initial state and noise either truncated Gaussians (±3σ) or uniform. The unsafe events are x2 ≤ −1.5 and the tail x2 ≤ −2.5.

  • With truncated noise, the zonotent was inside the 99% Monte Carlo intervals at all but one of the 502 pairs of a step and an event, and the untruncated Gaussian agreed with it to within 10−12.
  • With uniform noise, the zonotent fell outside the intervals at 80 and 69 of the 251 steps.
  • With uniform noise and the initial box as a set, the probabilistic-zonotope bound exceeded one at 39 steps, and with the box hull of Althoff et al. it was at most 0.89.

The zonotent takes its accuracy from the exact mean and covariance, as the untruncated Gaussian does, and like the Gaussian it carries no guarantee, so its density serves the reduction, which needs it to choose the part of the support to discard. The hull of a probabilistic zonotope puts the peak density of its Gaussian part at every point of its zonotope, so its bound exceeds one, and enclosing the box removes this at the price of a loose bound. E3

Guaranteed bounds through first exit

Does the first-exit bound hold for the true system while certifying a narrower gap and accepting faster moves of an open cup of water than worst-case reachability?

Both parts propagate a Gaussian overbound and bound the probability that the true state ever leaves a sequence of sets, which are the reduced supports for the narrow gap and the states that do not spill for the cup.

Narrow gap. A 0.5 m × 0.4 m robot passes between two obstacles. With the reduced supports as the sets, the bound is the sum of the masses discarded by the reductions. Every reduction met its budget of ζ = 10−3 at every step up to t = 12, so each guaranteed bound on collision was 0.012.
