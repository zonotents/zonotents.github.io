Reachability analysis usually propagates either sets, which bound where a system can go but not how likely each state is, or Gaussians, which assign probability to states the system can never reach. A zonotent pairs a zonotope with a probability density supported on it.

We focus on Gaussian zonotents, whose density is a Gaussian truncated to the zonotope. They propagate exactly through affine maps and approximately, by moment matching, through Minkowski sums. A propagated zonotope gains generators at every step, so the main contribution is probabilistic order reduction, which uses the density to discard parts of the support that carry little probability. Its outer form encloses the support, and its inner and free forms keep all but a chosen share of the probability, with bounds that hold through exact propagation.

In experiments, Gaussian zonotents matched Monte Carlo probabilities as closely as untruncated Gaussians; inner reductions of a Gaussian overbound certified, for the true system, a gap 42% narrower than the exact reachable set allows; and in carrying an open cup of water with an unknown fill depth, a relaxed Gaussian zonotent bounded the probability of spilling in a linear slosh model and accepted motions where worst-case reachability accepted none.

What is a zonotent?

A zonotope Z = Z(c, G) = \{c + G\beta : \|\beta\|_\infty \le 1\} is a robust set: it bounds every reachable state, and it stays closed under the affine maps and Minkowski sums of linear reachability. A zonotent pairs Z with a density f that integrates to 1 over Z. A Gaussian zonotent puts a scaled Gaussian on top of a uniform wall:

f(x) = w + \alpha\,\mathcal N(x;\mu,\Sigma)\ \text{ on } Z,w = \frac{\eta}{\operatorname{vol}(Z)},\qquad \alpha = \frac{1-\eta}{\nu(Z)},\nu(Z) = \int_Z \mathcal N(x;\mu,\Sigma)\,dx.

The wall share \eta \in [0,1] is the fraction of mass in the wall: \eta = 0 is a Gaussian truncated to Z, and \eta = 1 is the uniform zonotent. The mean \mu need not lie in Z, and (\mu, \Sigma) are parameters, not the density's own moments.

Zonotents can tell if the water will spill

A cup of water slides 40 cm across a table along a minimum-jerk path that takes τ seconds, and the fill depth is unknown (7.5 to 8.5 cm). A zonotent bounds the probability that the water spills during the whole carry, so a planner can pick the fastest move that meets a risk budget, such as at most a 1% chance of spilling. A zonotope alone contains every state the slosh model can reach, so it flags a spill even for moves that almost never spill. The zonotent's density shows that those states are unlikely. The fluid is simulated in 3D with Basilisk, and the simulation plays at quarter speed.

Minkowski sums by moment matching

Adding independent noise Y to a state X takes the Minkowski sum Z_1 \oplus Z_2 of the supports, and the density of X+Y is the convolution of the two densities, which is not a Gaussian zonotent. The moment-matched sum is the Gaussian zonotent on Z_1 \oplus Z_2, with no wall, whose own mean and covariance are exactly those of X+Y: \hat\mu_1+\hat\mu_2 and \hat\Sigma_1+\hat\Sigma_2. Its parameters are found by a fixed-point iteration. It carries no guarantee, so the demo compares it with the exact convolution.

Probabilistic order reduction

Every Minkowski sum adds generators, so the support of x_{t+1} = A x_t + w_t after t steps has 2 + 3t generators in the system below. Classical order reduction encloses the zonotope in a smaller one, which can only grow the support. Having a density lets us instead discard parts that carry little probability, with a budget \zeta:

  • Outer: Z \subseteq Z', here the interval hull of Z in the whitened frame of \Sigma. Nothing is discarded, and robust statements still hold.
  • Inner: Z' \subseteq Z keeps \int_{Z'} f \ge (1-\zeta). Every probability is then bracketed: \int_{U\cap Z'} f \le \int_U f \le \int_{U\cap Z'} f + \zeta. Here a maximum-volume template centered at the mode, shrunk to the budget. The budget can fail.
  • Free: the confidence parallelotope Z(\mu, r_\zeta L) with \Sigma = LL^\top, in closed form. When Z is symmetric about \mu, the Gaussian correlation inequality certifies that it keeps 1-\zeta of the probability.

Probability of collision

The probability that the state lies in an obstacle O is the zonotent's mass on Z_t \cap O, computed exactly for the propagated zonotent. Because the moment-matched sum is an approximation, this is an estimate, not a bound. The demo reproduces the collision part of E1: the system x_{t+1} = 0.85\,R(20^\circ)\,x_t + (0.6, 0.3) + w_t, a uniform initial state, and the obstacle of the paper, which you can drag.

Experiments

One validation and three experiments. All of them run in PyTorch in double precision on a laptop CPU.

Validation · Sec. VII-E

Order reduction across dimension

Which reductions meet their budget along propagated states from ℝ2 to ℝ10, and what does each cost in volume, accuracy, and time?

One random linear system per dimension (spectral radius 0.9), with uniform or truncated Gaussian noise, propagated for 20 steps by moment-matched sums and reduced after every step with ζ = 10−3.

  • The free reduction met its budget at every step, in every dimension, and with both noise models, in 1 to 2 ms per reduction (lowest kept share 0.99894 among 240).
  • Outer reductions keep every state, but their volume compounds: up to 1.4×1012 times the 99.9% confidence ellipsoid in ℝ10.
  • Inner reductions met their budgets only in low dimensions, and with uniform noise missed them at every step from ℝ3 on.
  • Every moment-matched sum took at most 40 ms, on supports with up to 210 generators in ℝ10.

E1 · Sec. VIII-B

Collision probability and a narrow gap

Does a Gaussian zonotent keep the probabilities of collision of a Gaussian on a smaller support, and do inner reductions certify passage through a gap that the reachable zonotope blocks?

Collision. The largest errors of the moment-matched zonotent over t = 0…5 were 0.004 with uniform noise and 0.007 with truncated Gaussian noise, the same as those of the plain Gaussian. At t = 5 the support reduced after every step had area 0.52 with truncated Gaussian noise, against 2.56 for the exact reachable zonotope and 4.61 for its outer reduction.

Narrow gap. A 0.5 m × 0.4 m robot passes between two obstacles. Propagating a relaxed Gaussian overbound with a first-exit bound, every reduction met its budget at every step up to t = 12, so each guaranteed bound on collision was 0.012.

The guaranteed confidence ellipses certified about the same gap (0.55 m); the narrowest gap with no simulated collision in 106 trajectories was 0.61 m. Between 0.76% and 0.79% of trajectories left the reduced supports by t = 12, against the bound of 1.2%.

E2 · Sec. VIII-C

Probabilistic zonotopes with bounded noise

When the noise is bounded, how do zonotents compare with probabilistic zonotopes in their probabilities of unsafe sets?

The system of Althoff et al. (2009), discretized over 250 steps, with the initial state and noise either truncated Gaussians (±3σ) or uniform. Unsafe events x2 ≤ −1.5 and the tail x2 ≤ −2.5.

  • With truncated noise, the zonotent was inside the Monte Carlo intervals at all but one of the 502 pairs (largest errors 0.0010 and 0.0002), as was the plain Gaussian.
  • The probabilistic-zonotope upper bound exceeded the estimate by at most 0.017 and 0.0010; treating the noise as a set made it exceed one from step 21.
  • With uniform noise the zonotent fell outside the intervals at 80 and 69 of 251 steps (largest errors 0.0071 and 0.0016).
NoiseZonotent outside the 99% intervalLargest error, x2 ≤ −1.5 / tail
Truncated Gaussian1 of 502 pairs0.0010 / 0.0002
Uniform80 and 69 of 251 steps0.0071 / 0.0016

In short: with bounded noise the zonotent was as accurate as a plain Gaussian while its support also bounds the reachable states, and the guaranteed probabilistic-zonotope variant became vacuous. Neither the zonotent nor the plain Gaussian carries a guarantee here.

E3 · Sec. VIII-D

Slosh-aware transport of a cup of water

Does a relaxed Gaussian zonotent bound the probability of spilling an open cup of water while accepting faster moves than worst-case reachability and a Gaussian tail bound, and does its slosh model predict spills in a fluid simulation?

A linear pendulum slosh model (cup radius 3.5 cm, rim 10 cm, fill depth 7.5–8.5 cm unknown) with noisy initial slosh and acceleration error. Each method picks the shortest 40 cm move whose spill probability is at most pmax.

  • The zonotent bound was at least the Monte Carlo estimate for all 282 plans, by a factor of 1.6 to 2.5 where the estimate lay between 10−3 and 0.1.
  • Worst-case reachability accepted no plan: its support reached at least 3.0 times the threshold.
  • In 3D Basilisk fluid simulations, the model agreed on whether the water spilled in all 33 runs on 2.5 mm cells and in 27 of 30 on 1.25 mm cells.
